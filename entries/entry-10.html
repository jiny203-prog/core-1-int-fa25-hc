Drift Hour

Afternoon arrives without warning. The light turns slower, softer, almost like it’s thinking about leaving. Shadows stretch long across the floor, and the room starts to feel heavier. Everything that was too bright in the morning is now faded, dulled by the weight of time.

I should be doing something—writing, working, answering messages—but my body has learned how to move without direction. The cursor blinks. The clock hums. My thoughts drift in quiet circles, looping back to the same starting point again and again. I keep waiting for a spark that never comes.

The air feels warm and still, like the moment before a storm or the pause between breaths. There’s no sound but the low buzz of daylight slipping away. Even my phone stays silent, as if it knows this hour doesn’t belong to anyone. It’s just me, the room, and the slow fading of everything.

Sometimes I think about how many afternoons have passed like this—unnoticed, unrecorded, quietly dissolving into evening. They pile up behind me like a long blur, indistinguishable from one another. Maybe that’s what living through insomnia does: it stretches time until all the days blend into one long, tired line.

I stand by the window for a while. The sky outside is pale and distant, almost silver. For a second, I think I see movement—a bird, a leaf, a shadow—but it’s gone before I can be sure. The world keeps turning even when I don’t feel it.

The light fades a little more, and I realize I’ve spent the whole day waiting for something to change. But the truth is, nothing ever really shifts at once. It all drifts, slowly, like dust in warm air. And before I notice, day becomes night again, and the cycle starts over—quiet, endless, weightless.
